I found these words carved into a broken slab of stone, half-buried in moss where the roots claw into the cliffs above the river. The lines are uneven, as though the carver struck in fever or trance. At the bottom, in a cramped hand, is the date: the year 382. I have copied the words here as best I could:

“In twenty years’ time, the heavens shall fail.
The folk of the Shadowed Forest shall name it the Endless Night.
No dawn shall come, no fire shall warm, no star shall comfort.
Not crown nor vengeance shall call it down,
but the stretching of thought beyond its bounds,
the hunger to know what should lie veiled.
A question shall be asked,
and the stars themselves shall break their silence to answer.
Woe to the questioner, woe to the answer!
For what is given shall not be unspoken.
The roots shall drink of it, the stones shall whisper it.
Night shall linger though the sun has risen.
Take heed, wanderer!
Ask not of the stars, lest they hear you.
Knock not at the gate of knowing,
lest it open wide and devour.”

I found this journal bound in cracked leather, the spine stiff with mildew. Most pages are unreadable, but a handful remain. The hand is heavy, the ink smudged, as though written by a man who preferred spear and snare to quill. I have copied one entry here:

“The forest grows stranger with every season. I have hunted these glades for half my life, yet the game no longer runs as it once did. A great plague has come upon the beasts — not the wasting sickness we knew before, but something fouler. Wolves born with too many teeth, stags whose hides split and crawl, men whispering that their kin howl beneath the moon. Folk in Chicken’s Rest call it the plague of beastmen, and I have seen enough with my own eyes to name it true.

They say this scourge came after the long dark, that it followed the Endless Night, like carrion follows battle. I have marked it first in my hunts around the year four hundred and thirty, when the woods would no longer give clean kill, only monsters.

Some say it is the work of Ashroot, the Rootbinder. But I know better. I tracked the plague to the northern glens, far from her groves, and saw her signs untouched there. If it were Ashroot’s weaving, the brambles themselves would have risen in fury, yet they lay still. Whatever curse this is, I found no sign it was Ashroot”.

Dated: The year four hundred and five, from the Collegium at Greymire

“My dear colleague,

You asked me to keep a record of notable disturbances in the northern wood, and I do so now with no small urgency. A traveler of uncommon bearing has crossed the vale — not man, not beast, but some elder power newly come among us. Do not be deceived by the names whispered in tavern and campfire. I tell you plainly: it is Nytheris, the Pale Reflection, She-Who-Covets. She is no invention of peasants’ fear. I studied her mention in the Codex Tenebrae, and I know the marks.

Already the signs match the texts: mirrors fogged in daylight, voices repeating in a tone colder than one’s own, fires dimming when she draws near. These are her powers, always the same — she takes what belongs to others and hollows it. The songs of bards turn to silence on their lips, the prayers of priests return unanswered, the courage of hunters crumbles as they see their own faces sneer back from still water. She is terrible not for what she creates, but for what she unmakes.

You asked her purpose. It is not hidden. I saw it in the way she lingered at Ashroot’s groves, staring until leaves browned on the branch. I heard it in the way she mocked Thistlebarrow’s horn, calling its dawn-song a childish cry. I even found her standing beneath the stone cairn of the Endless Night, tracing its runes as though wishing the darkness were her deed. These things she envies, and envy is the compass that guides her steps. Know this: envy is her nature, and envy her guide. She will not rest until her name eclipses all others.

You must leave the Shadowed Woods at once. She first revealed herself here this very year, when the forest is still weakened by old calamities, and slipped her shadow among them. Now she searches for a ruin to claim as her own.

Do not write me again from Chicken’s Rest. If you remain, she will find you — and once she knows what you cherish, she will take it, and leave you with nothing but your own hollow reflection.”

Extract from the Catalogue of Unnatural Denizens of the Shadowed Forest

Entry XLVII: Ashroot, the Rootbinder

She is counted among the greater denizens of the Heart, seldom glimpsed but much feared. Reports describe her as a woman-shaped bole of bark and briar, crowned in thorn, her limbs rooting themselves into the soil as if the forest itself were her flesh. When she stirs, trees bend toward her and undergrowth closes behind her like a gate.

Those who have faced her directly write of one certain dread: iron unsettles Ashroot. Even filings strewn at a threshold have held her back, and wounds cut by honest iron are said to still her song. Fire does not trouble her, nor silver — but iron gnaws at her essence.

As for her deeds, scholars have catalogued lesser blights in her wake: crops withering when her grief is heavy, herds vanishing as roots split open fields. Yet there remains bitter debate regarding the great calamities of the last century. Some villagers cry that the River of Blood was hers, while others accuse her of loosing the plague of beastmen. The editors here refrain from judgment, noting only that the signs recorded at those sites do not perfectly match her known habits. Attribution disputes persist.

In motive, the singers are strangely united. Every old song gives her the same sorrowful heart: Ashroot moves from grief, a lament for groves lost in ancient fire. Whether that grief makes her destroyer or protector, the catalogue leaves undecided.

Notes of the Collector

Doesn’t matter which village I go to, this is the only song that gets people to open their purses for me. I’m so sick of singing it I’ll never complain about a request again, so long as someone requests something other than this.

I asked someone what the dickens it means anyway and his answer didn’t make a lick of sense. Four three one is the date some bells broke in some backwater village around here. Downright creepy to have things like that going around. Soon as I have the coin I’m getting on the first ship back to Grand Harbor

To Nytheris,

You have in your grasp a book that does not belong to you. Do not deny it — the forest told me when you carried it past the Vale. The pages are lined with riddles of the heavens, questions that claw at my mind like briars. Return it, or lend it, but do not let it rot beneath your jealous keeping.

I have no lust to rule as mortals do, no wish to heap crowns or weigh myself with gold. It is curiosity that drives me, the gnawing thirst to split open mystery and taste what hides beneath. When I have my answers I will return your grimoire with no complaints, but that Codex holds an answer I will have, whether you will it or not.

Do not mistake my asking for weakness. I know your tether, Nytheris. The Ancient Mask waits in its cairn. It remembers the faces it devoured, and it would strip away every veil you wear until only the beast beneath remained. If I must strike its waters to loosen your grip, I will. Better you part with the Codex willingly.

The forest bends to antler and root, not to envy. Consider well before you test which grows deeper: your shadow or my horn.

—Thistlebarrow, Lord of Horn and Branch

How amusing you are, stomping through the forest with your demands. You claw at shadows for a book you cannot reach — and how it thrills me to hold it only because you crave it so.

Yes, your precious book rests with me. Do I study it? No. Its riddles bore me, its ink stinks of dust. It sings only because it calls to you. I’ve not been long in this land so you may not know me that well, but hear me now: Envy is my crown, and nothing pleases me more than to snatch from others what they most desire.

Would you like it? I could be persuaded. What price will you pay, stag? A grove bled white, a trove of antler-shed, or perhaps something simpler… your company, while I watch you strain against your hunger. The thought of you bent low, begging for scraps, almost makes me generous. Almost.

But lest you forget: the Mirror-Lake knows your reflection. Its waters would drink your strength, drag you beneath, and hush your proud bellow into silence. I could let the forest know where to find it. How would you plead then? So don’t threaten me again, neither of us is the winner in that particular conflict

Write again, if you wish. Your letters amuse me, almost as much as your desperation does. Perhaps I’ll even answer. Perhaps I’ll name a price.

—Nytheris, Keeper of the Glass Veil

Field Notes: Observations on the Denizens of the Shadowed Forest

By Arvad Telen, Journeyman Naturalist of the Third Circle

Veydran (Corvus Dira): A carrion-bird of impossible size, wings dark as soot. Seen most often roosting near abandoned chapels. Its call is no true caw but the toll of a bell, heard even where no tower stands. Local belief holds that the bells fell silent at his passing, and many fled the forest thereafter. Fear seems to trail him — some whisper it is both his nature and his cause. His form falters near Ashes of the First Fire, which the old rites say burn still at hidden altars.

Ashroot (Radix Umbra): A vast knot of roots rising from the ground like the ribs of a buried beast. Said to speak in dreams, commanding flame and bloom alike. The villagers whisper of a fire that spread unnaturally quick, too swift for men’s torches — they blame Ashroot. Its growth recoils from radiant stones: the Sunstone Idol, in particular, is said to halt her spread. I have not tested this, nor do I dare. The great plant creature is content to sit deep in the heart and has no hunger to encroach outside it.

Nytheris (Speculum Malefica): Rarely glimpsed directly. More often one sees ripples in still water, or a reflection where no surface should cast one. The shepherds mark the plague of beast-shapes as her work, for since her arrival whole flocks have turned wild. I cannot verify this. Still, she prowls where envy festers — that much is certain. The Ancient Mask is said to unmake her illusions, revealing the beast below. Such knowledge is dangerous even to record.

Cindralok (Serpens Cinis): A serpent coiled of soot and ember. Scales smolder when the moon wanes. I’ve read a childrens story where Cindralok is bound by silver chains, though I cannot fathom how anyone could get close enough to the fiery creature to bind him.

I close this entry with reluctance. These are not specimens, but terrors. To set quill to their names is to invite them to notice. I only hope they find me beneath their attention

Chronicle of the Shadowed Years

By Edda, Daughter of Sern, Scribe of Emberholt, dated this year: 435

In the spring of 399, the Northern Orchards bore fruit out of season. Apples hung fat and red though frost still lay on the ground.

But in the autumn of that same year, the Great Wildfire swept the eastern wood. It leapt tree to tree with unnatural speed, and hunters fled the vale. All settlements in its path were destroyed before it was stopped in greenbelt by a fine chain of silver hung round the town.

In the summer of 402, the Endless Night began. No sunrise came for three months, only a sky thick with stars.

In the winter that followed, children dreamed of silver paths. Three hunters, long lost, returned by maps drawn from those dreams.

In the spring of 403, the Western Fields grew high grain beyond memory, stalks tall as a man’s shoulder. Few dared eat of it.

In the autumn of that year, the River ran red with blood. Fish choked the surface, and ferrymen laid down their poles.

In the early months of 404, the Bells of Orrenhold fell silent. The tower yet stood, but no sound came of it, only the circling cries of black birds.

But by midsummer, healers gathered herbs of rare virtue from a clearing lit with strange fire. For a season, the pox in Ashen Cross eased.

In the years following came lesser signs: lambs born twisted, wells turned bitter, flocks that would not be herded.

Yet too, there was mercy. In one autumn, golden gourds swelled on the vines beyond all knowing; in another spring, motes of light drifted through the air, and those who breathed them claimed their fevers broke.

Five winters past, the Plague of Beastmen began. At first a shepherd, then a mason, then households entire. They walk the streets even now. I bar my door at night.

These past fifty years has held more unnatural phenomena than the hundred years prior. Do the beings of the old magic respect mortal calendar events like the turn of the century? Or did something else cause them to start acting so frequently? Our survival may depend on the answer.

The Answer

A letter found folded into the back of Edda's chronicle, in a hand not her own.

Edda,

You asked at the end of your chronicle whether the old powers keep mortal calendars. They do not. Here is what your fifty years truly record.

In the year 382 a stone-carver wrote it plainly: in twenty years a question would be asked, and the stars would break their silence to answer. And in 402 — twenty years on, as the carver promised — Thistlebarrow, Lord of Horn and Branch, asked his question of the heavens, and the Endless Night fell. Not crown nor vengeance called it down: curiosity did. The hunger to know what should lie veiled. He has not stopped asking since; it is why he claws at the Codex even now.

The rest of your chronicle is the ancient ones at work, each by their own nature. Cindralok, the serpent of soot and ember, set the Great Wildfire — stopped only by a chain of silver, as the children's story remembers. Veydran the crow silenced the Bells of Orrenhold, for the bells were broken by their fear, and fear wears a feathered skin. And Nytheris — the Pale Reflection, She-Who-Covets, new-come to the forest in 405 — is the author of the plague of beastmen. She takes what belongs to others and hollows it; she hollowed your neighbors out of their shapes. The hunter was right that it was not Ashroot. The naturalist's shepherds were right that it was her. Envy is her crown.

So the stag asks, the serpent burns, the crow frightens, the rootbinder grieves — and the pale one covets. They will not stop. They will never stop.

One mercy remains. The Ancient Mask waits in its cairn, and it holds the power to bind or banish the author of the plague. It remembers the faces it devoured; it would strip away every veil Nytheris wears until only the beast beneath remained. Find it, and the town may yet bar its doors for the last time.

Ask not of the stars, lest they hear you.
