The Stag's Own Verse — a dawn-song of the hill-country, sung to the Lord of Horn and Branch. Bards give it at the turning of the year.

The Stag's Own Verse

Fire has its serpent, and the flood its fish,
the deep stone keeps its iron king below;
the soul is stalked, the sleeping frame is blessed —
but hear now where the horned lord chose to go.

Of all the powers, one and one alone
belongs to the open Air, and it is he:
Thistlebarrow, the stag, is the lord of the wind and the sky,
and no other god shares the Air with him.

Not flame, not flood, not stone, not stolen breath —
the four are spoken for, their masters known;
so what is left when all the rest are named
falls to the stag, and to the stag alone.

(a bard's note beneath the last verse:) Sing it slow. The joke of the song is that you already know the answer if you know the other four — the wind is simply what remains.

So lift the horn upon the dawn wind, friends,
and let the high air carry up his name:
the stag is of the Air, first, last, and always —
and every reagent unclaimed is his to claim.

Here ends the Stag's Own Verse.
