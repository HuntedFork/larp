Extract from A Bestiary of the Heart, being a naturalist's catalogue of the elder powers, their humours and their alchemical natures — copied from the notes of Arvad Telen, Journeyman Naturalist of the Third Circle, and continued by an unknown later hand.

Entry the First — Cindralok (Serpens Cinis)

The serpent coiled of soot and ember. I set him at the head of this catalogue not for love of him but because he is, of all the powers of the Heart, the least mistakable. Where the others hide their natures behind riddle and reflection, Cindralok is fire and nothing but fire — a creature whose every breath, scale, and shed skin belongs wholly to the element of Flame. His scales are said to smolder when the moon wanes; the old rites reckon him bound only by silver chains, though I cannot fathom the courage of the smith who forged them. Where he passes, the eastern woods still bear the char of the Great Wildfire. Let the reader hold this fast: whatever else is doubtful in these pages, of Cindralok's element there is no doubt at all.

(margin, later hand:) Tested by the burning of the orchards. He is Flame. Do not test it again.

A comparative aside, on the Lord of Horn and Branch

It is the failing of lesser catalogues to name a creature only by what it is. The careful student learns as much from what a creature is not. Consider Thistlebarrow, the great stag — for the peasants forever confuse the elder powers one with another, and I would spare my reader that error.

They will tell you the stag kindled the wildfire; he did not. No spark of flame answers to the stag's tread. They will tell you the River of Blood was his doing; but still water and flood are strangers to him, and he holds no dominion over the drowning deep. They will point to the standing stones and swear the stag raised them; yet stone and the buried ore of the earth are no kin of his, and the deep places know nothing of his name. And in their fear they will even whisper that it was Thistlebarrow who hollowed the minds of the beastfolk; this is the cruelest error of all, for the stag has no purchase on the soul or spirit of any living thing — that theft belongs to another, and he neither covets nor keeps what dwells within.

What, then, is the stag? That I leave to a truer song than mine. I mark here only the four doors closed to him, and trust the reader to find the one that remains open.

(margin, later hand:) Four crossed out — one left. Think on which.
